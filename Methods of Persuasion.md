# Methods of Persuasion

This guide interprets “Methods of Persuasion 1–7” as Robert Cialdini’s seven principles of persuasion. Each section includes an original, hypothetical design example and a referenced example from research or a published account. The explanations connect the examples to the principles; study findings are specific to their settings, not guaranteed results for every website.

## 1. [Reciprocity](persuasion/reciprocity.md)

**Definition:** People tend to want to return a favor after receiving something valuable.

**Design example:** A cooking website offers a free meal-planning worksheet. Visitors may then be more willing to subscribe to its recipe newsletter.

**Referenced example — Restaurant candy and tipping:** Strohmetz and colleagues (2002) found that diners given a small chocolate with their bill tipped more than diners who received no candy. A second experiment found that both the amount of candy and the way it was offered affected tips.

**Connection to reciprocity:** The authors argued that receiving a small gift encouraged customers to give something back. For the cooking website, the comparable idea is to provide useful value before asking visitors to subscribe.

**Reference:** Strohmetz, D. B., Rind, B., Fisher, R., & Lynn, M. (2002). [Sweetening the Till: The Use of Candy to Increase Restaurant Tipping](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2002.tb00216.x). *Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 32*(2), 300–309.

## 2. [Scarcity](persuasion/scarcity.md)

**Definition:** People often value an opportunity more when its availability is limited.

**Design example:** A pottery shop displays the actual number of handmade mugs remaining beside the purchase button, helping customers understand availability.

**Referenced example — Concorde's retirement:** Influence at Work reports increased ticket sales after British Airways announced in 2003 that its Concorde service would end. This is a reported business example, not a controlled experiment.

**Connection to scarcity:** The opportunity was disappearing. The pottery shop similarly makes an actual availability limit visible.

**Reference:** Influence at Work. [The Science of Persuasion — Scarcity section](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/).

## 3. [Authority](persuasion/authority.md)

**Definition:** People are more likely to follow guidance from credible, knowledgeable experts.

**Design example:** An architecture firm's website places its architects' relevant qualifications and completed projects near its consultation form.

**Referenced example — Introducing an expert:** Influence at Work describes estate-agency receptionists mentioning colleagues' experience before transferring callers. The account reports more appointments and signed contracts afterward.

**Connection to authority:** Relevant experience gives customers a reason to trust advice. The architecture website can make genuine qualifications easy to evaluate.

**Reference:** Influence at Work. [The Science of Persuasion — Authority section](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/).

## 4. [Commitment and Consistency](persuasion/commitment-consistency.md)

**Definition:** People tend to act consistently with commitments they have already made, especially voluntary ones.

**Design example:** A language-learning website invites visitors to choose a daily study goal, then offers a practice schedule that supports their choice.

**Referenced example — Starting with a small request:** Freedman and Fraser (1966) studied the foot-in-the-door technique: asking for a small action before making a larger request. In their sign experiment, an initial small request increased later acceptance of a large roadside sign under several conditions compared with no initial request.

**Connection to commitment and consistency:** One interpretation is that an initial action can change how people see themselves, making a later action seem consistent with that identity. This illustrates the principle without proving that consistency is the only possible explanation. A self-chosen study goal can likewise give later practice a meaningful starting point.

**Reference:** Freedman, J. L., & Fraser, S. C. (1966). [Compliance without pressure: The foot-in-the-door technique](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Freedman_Fraser_Foot-in-the-door.pdf). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 4*(2), 195–202. DOI: `10.1037/h0023552`.

## 5. [Liking](persuasion/liking.md)

**Definition:** People are more receptive to requests from people they like or relate to.

**Design example:** A neighborhood bakery uses friendly copy, staff photographs, and an owner story to help visitors connect with the people behind the business.

**Referenced example — Rapport before negotiation:** Influence at Work summarizes MBA negotiation studies in which exchanging personal information and identifying common ground produced more agreements than immediately discussing business.

**Connection to liking:** Familiarity and rapport can make cooperation easier. The bakery's story offers a similar opportunity for personal connection.

**Reference:** Influence at Work. [The Science of Persuasion — Liking section](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/).

## 6. [Social Proof (Consensus)](persuasion/social-proof.md)

**Definition:** People look to others' behavior for guidance, particularly when they are uncertain.

**Design example:** A tutoring website shows authentic student reviews beside its booking options so prospective students can learn from others' experiences.

**Referenced example — Hotel towel reuse:** Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius (2008) tested messages asking hotel guests to reuse towels. Messages describing other guests' participation performed better than a standard environmental appeal in their experiments.

**Connection to social proof:** Information about what other people do can guide a decision. Student reviews serve a related purpose by showing prospective customers how others experienced a tutoring service.

**Reference:** Goldstein, N. J., Cialdini, R. B., & Griskevicius, V. (2008). [A Room with a Viewpoint: Using Social Norms to Motivate Environmental Conservation in Hotels](https://doi.org/10.1086/586910). *Journal of Consumer Research, 35*(3), 472–482.

**Research context:** A later replication by Bohner and Schlüter (2014) did not reproduce the original pattern consistently. The example demonstrates a reported effect whose strength depends on context. [A Room with a Viewpoint Revisited](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0104086), *PLOS ONE, 9*(8), e104086.

## 7. [Unity](persuasion/unity.md)

**Definition:** A shared group identity can make people more receptive to one another's influence.

**Design example:** A university alumni website highlights shared campus traditions and alumni stories alongside an invitation to join a mentoring program.

**Referenced example — Football supporters helping a stranger:** Levine and colleagues (2005) found that supporters were more likely to help an apparently injured stranger wearing their team's shirt than a rival team's or an unbranded shirt. In a second experiment, making a broader football-supporter identity salient extended helping to a rival-team supporter.

**Connection to unity:** A sense of shared membership can affect willingness to help. This is research on group identity and helping that illustrates unity, rather than an experiment originally labeled with Cialdini's seventh principle. The alumni example similarly invites participation through a shared identity.

**Reference:** Levine, M., Prosser, A., Evans, D., & Reicher, S. (2005). [Identity and Emergency Intervention: How Social Group Membership and Inclusiveness of Group Boundaries Shape Helping Behavior](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167204271651). *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 31*(4), 443–453.

**Framework reference:** Cialdini explains unity as shared identity in [Robert B. Cialdini and Jennifer L. Eberhardt on The 7 Principles of Influence](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/universal-principles-of-influence), published by the Association for Psychological Science.
